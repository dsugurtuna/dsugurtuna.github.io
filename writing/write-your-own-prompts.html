Write your own prompts: why borrowed libraries hollow out judgement

A prompt library hands you fluent output while quietly taking away the only part of the work that teaches you whether it is any good.

Ugur Tuna · 8 minute read

Every week, another glossy PDF does the rounds on LinkedIn promising “500 mega-prompts to master AI” or a button that writes the prompt for you before the model writes the work. Inside companies, well-meaning teams assemble shared prompt libraries so staff can copy, paste and move on.

I have come to dislike almost all of it.

Not because prompting does not matter — the opposite. Prompting is the single most important way a human being interacts with an AI system. Precisely because it matters so much, handing it over to a library or a prompt-writing bot is a quiet strategic mistake. When you borrow someone else’s prompt, you skip the exact step where your own mind clarifies what you actually want, and you weaken your ability to judge what comes back.

Why human nature loves a template

It is easy to see why prompt libraries sell so well. A blank text box is uncomfortable. It asks a sharp question that many of us would rather dodge for five more minutes: what am I actually trying to say, decide or build here?

Human beings do not carry finished thoughts inside their heads waiting to be transcribed. As the writing scholar Patricia Taylor puts it, we rarely have clean, direct access to our own thinking until we externalise it into concrete words we can inspect and challenge. That is why talking through a hard decision with a colleague — or drafting a clumsy first paragraph — suddenly reveals what was missing all along.

A copied mega-prompt spares you that friction. You drop three variables into somebody else’s clever scaffold, press enter, and three seconds later a polished page arrives. It reads cleanly. It sounds confident. And right there, the trap closes.

Taylor describes what happened when she fed her own detailed outline and past scripts into ChatGPT to tighten a conference talk. She regretted it the moment the text appeared. The draft did not sound like her, yet having prose that was “close enough” sitting on the screen made it harder, not easier, to see what she would have said herself. She compared it to an overbearing journal editor whose heavy rewrites left her alienated from her own argument. Anyone who has tried to rescue a bland AI draft built from a borrowed template knows that exact fog: the words on the page look respectable enough to silence your instincts, yet hollow enough that you no longer quite own them.

Playing with your own prompt is how you understand the output

When you write your own prompt — clumsily at first — something very different happens. You see the model miss your point. You realise you left out a constraint you took for granted. You tighten a sentence, cut a vague adjective, add a boundary case, and run it again. Now the second output shifts, and for the first time you can see why.

That back-and-forth play is not wasted motion. It builds a working mental model of both the machine and your own problem. You learn which instructions the model actually obeys, where it drifts, which requirements quietly pull against each other, and what a weak answer looks like in disguise.

The composer and researcher Kasia Głowicka captures this shift sharply. Looking at creative students using prompt-based AI, she noticed a recurring pattern: students could produce competent-looking work yet could not explain why they had chosen one direction over another. Without realising it, practitioners who simply pick from generated results slip “from creator to curator”. Drawing on her own years writing algorithmic music systems, she argues that you only truly collaborate with a machine when you shape the conditions of generation yourself — composing the system before composing with it. And when that feels awkward or slow, she makes a point every hurried executive should frame on the wall: cognitive friction is not evidence of clumsy tooling; it is evidence of skill being built.

Owning the production step means owning the check

Here is the second half of the problem, and in serious work it is the more dangerous half. Borrowing prompts does not just lower your involvement in creating; it lowers your involvement in checking.

Think about what happens when an analyst, a lawyer or a product manager copies a shared “board-memo prompt” or clicks “auto-optimise prompt”. Because they did not build the instructions piece by piece, they have no feel for what the prompt pushed the model to exaggerate, ignore or gloss over. The prompt is a black box feeding another black box. When the output arrives in tidy bullet points, the natural human response is to skim it, fix a typo and forward it.

Worse still is the lazy habit of telling an AI to refine itself on autopilot — “this is good, improve it” — without putting your own judgement back into the loop. A large EMNLP 2026 study by Shashidhar Reddy Javaji, Bhavul Gauri and Zining Zhu stress-tested exactly what happens when seven language models repeatedly try to “improve” their own work across twelve turns once the original grounding and human checks are stripped away. The results are sobering. On coding tasks, average unit-test pass rates collapsed from 40.7% on the first turn to 2.5% by the twelfth — even Claude Sonnet 4 fell from 74.4% to 2.0% — while the code bloated to nearly ten times its original length as the model piled on wrappers that broke working solutions. On open-ended ideation, telling models to keep making an idea “more novel” multiplied buzzwords twenty-five-fold (from 1.42 to 35.98 per response) while feasibility cratered from 6.68 to 1.69.

That experiment was an austere stress test, deliberately removing original context and external checks to isolate self-referential rewriting. Its lesson for real work is crystal clear: without a human holding the standard, inspecting concrete behaviour and steering each turn, iteration is not improvement. It is drift dressed up as polish.

What real prompt craft looks like in practice

Talk to engineering and product teams who actually ship reliable AI workflows, and almost none of them pulled a magic incantation out of a public library. Generic rules fail the moment they meet real human nuance — the difference between playful banter and harassment in a community forum, or the quiet commercial caveat that turns a plausible strategy memo into a reckless one.

In a CHI 2026 study of data-prompt co-evolution, Minjae Lee and Minsuk Kahng watched how people align prompts with their own judgement. When participants refined their own prompts against concrete edge cases — inspecting failures with their own eyes and stating in plain words why an answer was wrong — alignment with their personal standards rose meaningfully (reaching an F1 score of 0.69 against 0.56 for ad-hoc editing). Just as revealing was what happened in the unguided baseline: four out of sixteen participants edited their prompts in ways that produced zero change on real-world test cases. They thought they were steering the model; they were merely moving words around.

Testing your own prompt against real cases cures that illusion fast. You stop treating prompts like magic spells and start treating them like specifications you own.

Where examples help — and where the line sits

A fair objection is worth answering plainly. What about a beginner staring at a blinking cursor on day one, or someone working in a second language? Reading a handful of strong examples is genuinely useful. Nobody learns to write crisp briefs or clear emails in a vacuum. Seeing how someone else structures context, constraints and tone can break cold-start paralysis in five minutes.

The line is simple: read examples to learn the craft, then close the library and write your own. Treat a borrowed prompt the way a good chef treats a recipe book — something to study for technique, never a frozen meal you reheat and sign your name to. And if you work inside a team that needs shared system prompts for a production service, those prompts should be written, broken and audited by the people who own the outcome, backed by real test cases rather than copied wholesale from a blog post.

A simple habit for keeping ownership

If you want AI to sharpen your work rather than thin out your judgement, keep five small rules:

  1. Start in your own rough words. Write down what you are trying to achieve, who it is for and what a bad answer would look like before touching any template.
  2. Change one thing at a time. When an output disappoints you, tighten one constraint or add one real counter-example so you actually learn what moved the needle.
  3. Probe the boundaries. Push the prompt harder on tone, brevity or scepticism to see where the model breaks — you only understand a tool once you have seen its edges.
  4. Never tell an AI merely to “make it better”. Give a concrete critique from your own reading: what is vague, what is missing, what claim lacks support.
  5. Stay inside the production step. Use AI to expand options, pressure-test ideas and speed up drafting, while keeping the definition of “good” and the final check firmly in your own hands.

Prompting is not an administrative chore to outsource so you can get to the “real work” faster. In the age of AI, writing and refining your own prompt is the real work. Give that step away, and you may still produce plenty of pages — you just will not quite know whether any of them deserve your name.
