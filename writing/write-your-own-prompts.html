Writing

Why you should write your own prompts

Prompt libraries and prompt generators save a minute of typing and cost you your understanding of what the AI actually produced.

Ugur Tuna · 3 minute read

Prompting is the main way we talk to AI, yet a whole small industry now tries to stop people doing it for themselves. Everywhere you look, someone is sharing a giant prompt library or offering a tool that writes your prompt before the AI writes your answer. I stay away from both.

When you copy someone else’s long prompt and paste it in, a neat page of text arrives in seconds. On the surface that feels efficient. The trouble starts the moment you try to judge whether the page is actually right, or when someone asks why a section took a certain angle. Because you did not write the instructions, you do not know what those instructions quietly pushed the model to assume, stretch or leave out. You have become a bystander in your own work.

Writing the prompt is how you work out what you mean

Most of us do not sit down at a desk with a finished thought ready to type. We find out what we really think by trying to put it into plain words and seeing where it sounds weak or incomplete. A blank prompt box asks a useful question: what am I actually trying to get done here, and what would count as a good result?

Skipping that step has a strange effect on the human mind. Even when someone feeds an AI their own notes to turn into a finished script, seeing a draft that is “close enough” on the screen can make it harder to hear what they would have said themselves. Once smooth, borrowed wording sits in front of you, your own voice gets crowded out. Editing becomes harder rather than easier because you are trying to rescue text you never fully owned.

Playing with your prompt teaches you how the model behaves

Writing two or three rough sentences of your own works very differently. The first reply comes back and misses part of what you wanted. You read it, spot the gap, change one instruction, and run it again. Maybe you tighten a vague word, add an example, or state a limit you forgot to mention.

That back-and-forth play is where real understanding comes from. After a few tries, you start to see why the model gave you that output. You notice where it takes shortcuts, where it sounds confident without facts, and which rules it tends to drop when the task gets long. People who only pick from ready-made prompts often produce tidy results without being able to explain why one version works and another fails. Little by little, they stop acting as creators and turn into passive selectors.

The same problem shows up when people ask an AI to “improve” a draft over and over without putting their own thinking back in. In recent tests across seven models, telling an AI to keep improving its own code across twelve rounds without fresh human direction broke almost all of the working answers and made the code nearly ten times longer. Asking it to make ideas “more novel” without clear guidance simply piled on buzzwords while making the ideas less practical. Without a human steering each step, repetition just drifts.

Own the production step

Looking at a few example prompts when you first try a new tool is harmless, much like reading a good letter before writing your own. The mistake is living out of someone else’s template book.

Using AI well does not mean stepping back from how the work is made. It means staying right inside the production step. Write your own prompt in plain words, play with it until you see how the model responds, and check the result with eyes that know exactly what was asked for in the first place.
