Why you should write your own prompts

Borrowed prompt libraries hide the assumptions inside an output. Writing your own specification—and co-evolving test cases beside it—keeps you in control of the work.

Ugur Tuna · 22 November 2026 · 3 minute read

A whole cottage industry sells giant prompt libraries and automated prompt generators. Both save a minute of typing. In practice, both create the same operational blind spot.

Paste someone else’s three-hundred-word prompt and tidy text arrives in seconds. The trouble starts when a colleague asks why one paragraph made an odd leap or skipped a caveat. Because you did not write the instructions, you do not know what they told the model to stretch or omit. You have become a bystander in your own work.

Writing the specification forces intent

Drafting your own instructions forces three clear choices upfront: what sources the model may use, what constraints it must obey, and what counts as a failure. Skipping that step dulls judgement even on familiar material. In June 2026, writing researcher Patricia Taylor fed ChatGPT her own detailed outline and prior scripts [1]. Seeing smooth prose that felt “close enough” made it harder, not easier, to hear what she actually wanted to say.

At CHIWORK ’26, Kasia Głowicka documented a similar shift [2]. Practitioners relying on canned templates produced neat artefacts yet struggled to explain why one choice beat another. Rescuing borrowed prose takes longer than writing two plain, testable lines yourself.

Blind “improve this” loops break code and reasoning

Handing iteration back to the model without fresh human criteria fails just as badly. In an EMNLP 2026 study, Shashidhar Reddy Javaji, Bhavul Gauri, and Zining Zhu tested seven models over twelve self-refinement turns with no new external checks [3].

On Python coding tasks, unit-test pass rates fell from 40.7% on turn one to 2.5% by turn twelve. Claude Sonnet 4 dropped from 74.4% to 2.0%, while code length grew nearly tenfold. When asked to make ideas “more novel”, models multiplied buzzwords twenty-five times over while practical feasibility collapsed [3]. Without human failure criteria, repetition simply drifts.

Co-evolve the prompt and the test set

Good prompting does not stop in a chat window. In CHI ’26 work on Data-Prompt Co-Evolution, Minjae Lee and Minsuk Kahng showed why prompts and test sets have to grow together [4]. Each time you spot an edge-case failure—a dropped null result, a mangled unit, an invented citation—you fix the instruction and promote that input into a fixed test suite.

For engineering teams, prompts belong in version control beside schemas and refusal rules. In evidence-synthesis-eval, each rubric file stamps its version and SHA-256 hash into every score record so edits stay traceable:

prompts/claim_support.v1.md  # SHA-256 hashed in every report
  1. Read ONLY the claim sentence and cited S1..Sn text.
  2. Ignore outside world knowledge when grading traceability.
  3. Output strict JSON: verdict, quoted_evidence, rationale.
  4. Never guess on unparseable output: log as judge failure.

Four habits that keep you in charge

  1. Draft from scratch: state scope, permitted sources, output schema, and what the model must refuse to guess.
  2. Isolate every edit: change one rule at a time so you see what shifted the output.
  3. Never ask to “make it better”: name the exact defect and supply the counter-example.
  4. Version prompts beside tests: promote every surprise failure into a permanent regression check [4].

Reading examples on day one is fine. Keeping your own hands on the specification—and on the tests that verify it—is what makes AI output defensible.
