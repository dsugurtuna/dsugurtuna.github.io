Writing

Write your own prompts

Prompt libraries hand you smooth text and take away the step where you learn whether the answer is actually right.

Ugur Tuna · 4 minute read

I do not use prompt libraries, and I never click buttons that offer to write my prompt for me.

Watch what happens when someone copies a three-hundred-word prompt off the internet and pastes it into a model. Five seconds later they have two pages of tidy prose. Then a colleague points to paragraph four and asks why it makes a strange assumption. They cannot answer. They did not write the instructions, so they have no idea what those instructions told the model to invent, exaggerate or leave out.

Prompting is the main point of contact between your mind and the machine. Giving that step away saves thirty seconds of typing and costs you your grip on the work.

Why borrowed words block your own thinking

People reach for templates because a blank box is uncomfortable. Writing your own prompt forces you to decide what you actually want before the machine speaks.

Most of us do not know our full thought until we try to put it into plain words on a page. The writing professor Patricia Taylor tested this on herself in June 2026 while preparing a short conference talk. She fed ChatGPT her own detailed outline and past scripts, asking it to turn them into a tight draft. She regretted it the moment the text appeared. It did not sound like her, yet having a version that was “close enough” sitting in front of her made it harder, not easier, to hear what she would have said herself.

Borrowed prompts create that fog every day. They hand you respectable-looking filler fast enough to silence your own judgement.

Tinkering is how you learn what the model is doing

Start with two rough sentences of your own instead. Read what comes back. Notice where the model took a lazy shortcut or missed a constraint you forgot to state out loud. Change one line and run it again.

By the third pass, two things happen at once. Your own thinking gets sharper, and you start to see the edges of the model. You learn where it invents facts, which words push it too far, and what needs checking line by line.

The composer and researcher Kasia Głowicka saw the cost of skipping that struggle in her students. Many could generate competent-looking work with AI yet could not explain why they picked one approach over another. Without noticing, they had slipped from creator to curator. When reworking a prompt feels slow or awkward, that friction is not wasted time. It is the exact moment skill is forming.

Blind “make it better” loops break good work

Letting AI polish its own output without fresh human direction fails just as badly. In an EMNLP 2026 study, Shashidhar Reddy Javaji, Bhavul Gauri and Zining Zhu ran a twelve-turn stress test across seven models, stripping away the original task and outside checks after turn one to see what happens when a model repeatedly tries to “improve” its own answer. On Python tasks, unit-test pass rates fell from 40.7% on turn one to 2.5% by turn twelve (Claude Sonnet 4 dropped from 74.4% to 2.0%), while code ballooned nearly tenfold in length. Told to make ideas “more novel”, models multiplied buzzwords twenty-five times over while practical feasibility collapsed.

Iteration only improves work when a person stays inside the production step, checks concrete cases and states plainly what went wrong.

Where examples fit, and where they stop

Reading a few good prompts on day one is fine, especially if you are new to a tool or working in a second language. Study them the way you would read a well-written brief to pick up structure. Then close the tab and write your own words.

Keep the habit simple:

  1. Write your first prompt from scratch, however rough.
  2. Change one rule at a time so you see what caused the shift in output.
  3. Never type “improve this”; name the exact flaw you spotted with your own eyes.
  4. Treat AI as help inside a production step you still own from start to finish.

If you did not write the question and wrestle with the revisions, you are only signing your name to a stranger’s recipe.
