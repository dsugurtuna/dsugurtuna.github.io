AI is not a coding tool: why the biggest value sits outside the IDE

The AI industry talks as though language models exist mainly to write software. Most of the people actually using them are thinking through strategy, learning, law and everyday decisions.

Ugur Tuna · 7 minute read

Follow the mainstream AI conversation for a week — keynote launches, technology podcasts, investor letters, benchmark leaderboards — and you could be forgiven for thinking artificial intelligence is a specialised machine for writing Python and JavaScript. Every release celebrates a new record on a coding benchmark. Every viral clip shows someone building an app in ninety seconds.

Yet step outside Silicon Valley’s engineering bubble and look at how most human work actually happens. Lawyers do not spend their weeks writing unit tests. Hospital directors, board chairs, teachers, policy advisers, negotiators and founders spend their lives wrestling with strategy, learning unfamiliar fields, reading contracts and regulations, weighing commercial trade-offs and sharpening critical thinking.

AI is not a coding tool that happens to speak English. It is a reasoning and dialogue partner that also happens to write code. Treating it primarily as software tooling leaves most of its real strategic value sitting untouched on the table.

Why the spotlight stays glued to code

There is nothing mysterious about why the AI labs talk endlessly about programming. It is a textbook supply-side incentive, not a mirror of human need.

As Mark Sullivan laid out in Fast Company in June 2026, big labs bet heavily on AI coding for three interlocking reasons. First, companies already spend vast budgets on software engineering and will pay immediately for tools that speed developers up — welcome revenue for labs spending billions ahead of potential public listings. Second, code is unusually tidy training material: unlike human strategy or legal judgement, which are messy and arguable, code compiles and passes tests or it fails, giving researchers a clean, measurable right answer. Third, labs are chasing a deeper prize — coding agents strong enough to help improve the code behind the AI models themselves.

All three reasons make total sense for an AI lab. None of them means coding is where the rest of society gets the greatest leverage.

What people actually do when nobody is filming a demo

Look past the keynotes to real usage data, and the coding-only narrative breaks down almost immediately — even on the very platform developers love most.

In June 2026, Anthropic published its Cadences Economic Index report, measuring what people actually walk away with from conversational Claude chat and Cowork sessions (separate from dedicated developer tools such as Claude Code and direct API traffic). Code and technical work accounted for only about one-sixth of outputs. Roughly a third of everything people produced were conversational outputs — explanations (17%) and guidance (11%) — and another third were written deliverables such as documents and reports (15%), presentations, emails and strategic plans.

Even more human was the rhythm of the clock. Business correspondence peaked at ten and eleven in the morning. Dinner recipes spiked more than twofold at six in the evening. Sleep advice peaked in the quiet hours before dawn. On 14 April, U.S. tax questions surged eightfold in a single day. And over the weekend, while routine office tasks faded, conversations about starting a business climbed to their weekly peak. In Anthropic’s linked survey of nearly ten thousand users, almost a quarter (23%) were managers — more than three times their share of the wider workforce — using AI to draft memos, test analyses and sharpen plans while keeping human judgement and trust firmly on their side of the desk. Nearly seven in ten (68%) said they were learning more with AI.

Widen the lens to the world’s largest consumer AI platform and the same pattern holds globally. OpenAI’s October 2026 Signals release mapped work-related ChatGPT messages across nearly a hundred countries and 165 distinct occupational activities — from preparing instructional materials and marketing content to analysing business data and programming. Far from narrowing into coding, work usage was actively diversifying: across seventy-four countries tracked every quarter, the share taken by the ten most common activities shrank from 56.0% in early 2025 to 51.1% by late 2026.

Strategy, learning, law and critical thinking

Why is talking through non-coding problems with AI so potent? Because human beings rarely suffer from a shortage of information; we suffer from blind spots, rushed framing and the sheer effort of holding ten competing variables in our heads at once.

Used properly, a frontier model becomes an infinitely patient sparring partner across domains that never touch an IDE:

  • Strategy: Lay out your market thesis and ask the model to attack it as a ruthless competitor, a cash-strapped customer or a sceptical regulator. Weak assumptions crack in ten minutes instead of ten months.
  • Learning: Ask for an explanation of genomic imputation, sovereign cloud law or derivative pricing calibrated to what you already know, then interrogate every step until you can explain it back in plain words.
  • Law and governance: Map obligations across a dense contract or regulatory framework, flag ambiguous clauses and list what questions to put to qualified legal counsel — turning hours of first-pass reading into focused human scrutiny.
  • Critical thinking: Expose hidden contradictions in your own memo, test whether your conclusion actually follows from your premises, and rehearse tough boardroom questions before you walk into the room.

Notice something vital about every single one of those uses. None of them is a one-click command. In Anthropic’s Cadences data, writing an article in conversational chat took a median of thirteen rounds of back-and-forth, compared with a single prompt in a coding agent. And higher-paid occupations did not shrink human effort — they expanded dialogue, staying in conversation 1.53 times as many turns as lower-wage work. Thinking deeply with AI is an active, conversational craft.

The mirror effect: your question sets the ceiling

Anthropic’s January 2026 Economic Index report revealed two findings that every non-technical leader should read twice. First, across 117 countries and 50 U.S. states, the intellectual sophistication of the model’s answer tracked the sophistication of the human’s prompt almost one-for-one (correlations of 0.925 and 0.928). Ask a shallow question and you get a shallow answer; bring real domain rigour and sharp framing, and the model rises to meet you. Second, moving from isolated single-turn API calls to multi-turn human conversation stretched the complexity of tasks Claude could handle reliably from roughly 3.5 hours of human-equivalent work to an extrapolated 19 hours — because each human turn breaks a messy problem down and steers it back on course.

That is why non-coders who bring deep judgement in law, medicine, finance, education or leadership often get more transformative leverage from AI than developers realise. In fact, Anthropic’s data showed larger estimated speedups on college-level analytical tasks (around twelvefold) than on simpler high-school-level tasks (around ninefold), highlighting complex non-coding work such as legal research on court decisions and statutory rules.

Giving coding its due without letting it own the room

To be completely fair, AI coding is not hype. Software engineering is genuinely being reshaped, and dedicated coding agents inside terminals and IDEs deliver real productivity gains where automated tests verify the result. Coding deserves its seat at the table.

The mistake is mistaking one loud room for the whole house. When an organisation treats AI strategy as “buy licences for the engineering team and see what happens”, the legal team, the commercial strategists, the clinicians, the educators and the executive board are left standing in the hallway — even though their decisions move the entire business.

Bringing AI into the rest of your thinking

If your team — or your own daily habit — still files AI under “technical tools”, try four immediate shifts:

  1. Bring your next messy decision, not your next script. Spend twenty minutes arguing out a real strategic trade-off before you open a slide deck.
  2. Use it to learn one adjacent discipline properly. Have it quiz you, correct your mental model and walk you through real counter-examples.
  3. Train non-technical teams on dialogue, not tricks. Teach lawyers, managers and analysts how to question, challenge and verify answers against primary sources — never as a substitute for professional responsibility, always as an amplifier for judgement.
  4. Measure value beyond engineering velocity. Better decisions, faster onboarding into complex fields, clearer governance and earlier detection of blind spots rarely show up in lines of code, yet they are where enduring advantage lives.

Code is only one dialect of human problem-solving. Most of the choices that shape a career, a company or a society happen in plain language — through argument, evidence, learning and judgement. The sooner we stop treating AI as a coding machine and start using it as a partner for serious thinking across every discipline, the closer we get to what this technology is actually for.
