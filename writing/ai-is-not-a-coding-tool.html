Beyond code: what most people actually need from AI

Mainstream tech coverage treats AI as if it existed mainly to write code. Most of its real value sits in strategy, learning, law and critical thinking.

Ugur Tuna · November 2026 · 6 minute read

If you follow technology news, product launches or online debates about AI, you quickly get the impression that AI was built mainly for software engineers. Almost every big announcement centres on a coding benchmark. Almost every live demo shows an app or a website appearing from a short text instruction.

Coding is a great use of AI, and I write software myself. Still, treating AI first and foremost as a coding machine misses most of human life and most of human work. Outside engineering teams, people spend their days deciding strategy, learning hard subjects from scratch, reading legal documents, weighing risks, teaching students, running businesses and testing their own thinking. All of those happen in ordinary language, and AI can be extraordinary at every single one of them when used as a thinking partner.

Why the tech world talks so much about code

Before looking at everything beyond coding, it helps to ask why the big AI companies talk about programming morning and night. The answer has much more to do with how AI labs make money and train models than with what most people actually need.

First, software budgets are huge, and companies will pay straight away for tools that help developers ship faster. Second, code is unusually neat training material. In plain English, two sensible people can disagree all afternoon about whether a business strategy or a legal argument is sound. Code is cleaner: either the programme runs and passes its tests, or it breaks. That clear right-or-wrong signal makes it much easier for researchers to train models on coding than on messy human judgement. Third, the labs themselves want AI helpers that can speed up the engineering behind their own next models.

Once you see those three incentives clearly, the spotlight makes sense. Code is convenient for the people building AI. That does not mean code is the whole story for the rest of us.

What people actually bring to AI

When you look past the stage demos to how millions of people actually use conversational AI across the week, a completely different picture shows up. Even on platforms best known among programmers, code and scripts make up only around a sixth of what people produce in everyday chat. Roughly two-thirds of outputs are explanations, advice, reports, plans and written documents.

The daily clock of real usage looks like ordinary human life rather than a software sprint. Work emails and memos peak mid-morning. Cooking questions jump at six in the evening. Tax questions surge eightfold right before filing deadlines. Over the weekend, office admin drops away and people sit down to plan new businesses of their own. Across nearly a hundred countries and well over a hundred different kinds of work, usage has grown wider over time rather than narrowing into tech jobs. Nearly a quarter of regular users in one large survey were managers, and roughly two in three said AI was helping them learn more.

Four places where talking with AI shines

The highest value outside coding rarely comes from telling AI to write a finished document while you make coffee. In real chat logs, writing a thoughtful piece takes a dozen rounds of back-and-forth, and people in higher-paid, judgement-heavy jobs stay in conversation noticeably longer than average. Four areas stand out whenever someone sits down to talk a problem through properly.

Strategy and decisions. When you are close to your own plan, it is hard to see its weak spots. Lay out what you are thinking of doing, state your real constraints, and ask the model to challenge the plan as a tough competitor, a doubtful board member or a careful customer who keeps saying no. Bad assumptions surface in fifteen minutes instead of six months later.

Learning unfamiliar fields. Textbooks and articles move at one fixed speed for every reader. In conversation, you can ask an AI to explain a medical study, an accounting rule or a branch of maths at your exact level, stop it whenever a term feels fuzzy, and ask it to quiz you until you can explain the whole idea back in your own words.

Law, contracts and policy. AI is not a lawyer and cannot take legal responsibility for you, yet it is a tireless first reader. You can walk through a dense contract or a new piece of regulation together, list every obligation and deadline, spot where two clauses pull in different directions, and walk into your meeting with qualified legal counsel carrying sharper questions.

Critical thinking. Perhaps the best everyday use of all is asking AI to read your own rough argument and point out where you jumped to a conclusion without evidence, where your logic goes in circles, or what strong counter-argument you brushed past too quickly.

The quality of your question sets the ceiling

One finding from large-scale usage studies deserves far more attention outside tech circles: the depth of an AI’s answer tracks the depth of the person’s prompt almost step for step. Ask a shallow, rushed question and you get generic, forgettable advice. Bring real domain knowledge, clear context and sharp follow-up questions, and the model operates at a completely different level. Even better, breaking a hard problem across a multi-turn conversation stretches the size of task a model can handle reliably from a few hours of one-off work to nearly a full working week’s complexity, because each human reply keeps steering the thinking back on track.

Engineers should carry on enjoying what AI does for code. Everyone else should stop assuming this technology belongs mainly to programmers. If your work involves weighing evidence, learning fast, reading carefully or making hard calls in plain English, AI is already sitting squarely in your field.
