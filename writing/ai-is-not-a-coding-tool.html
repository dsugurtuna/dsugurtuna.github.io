Writing

AI is for thinking, not just coding

Tech headlines treat AI as a machine for writing software. Its widest value sits in strategy, law, learning and critical thought.

Ugur Tuna · 4 minute read

Listen to how the tech industry talks about AI and you would think its main job on earth is writing software.

Launch videos show apps built in sixty seconds. Leaderboards rank models by Python puzzles. Meanwhile, most serious decisions in a hospital, a law firm, a boardroom or a government department never touch a code editor. They happen in plain language: testing whether a commercial plan holds water, spotting the trap in a contract clause, learning an unfamiliar field before Friday, or finding the weak link in your own argument before a rival does.

Filing AI under “developer tools” misses five-sixths of what people actually do with it.

Why AI labs talk about code all day

The coding obsession makes complete sense from inside an AI lab. As Mark Sullivan explained in Fast Company in June 2026, three incentives line up neatly. Companies already spend heavily on engineering and pay fast for developer speed. Code also gives clean training feedback: a script compiles and passes tests, or it fails, whereas a business strategy has no unit test. Finally, labs hope strong coding agents will help write better AI models next.

Those are good reasons for labs to build coding tools. They are terrible reasons for everyone else to narrow their imagination to software.

What real usage looks like outside the bubble

Even on Claude, long favoured by software engineers, conversational usage tells a much wider story. In Anthropic’s June 2026 Cadences report, code and technical scripts made up only about one-sixth of outputs in Claude chat and Cowork. Roughly a third were explanations and guidance, and another third were written deliverables such as reports, memos and strategic plans. Nearly a quarter of surveyed users (23%) were managers, and 68% said they were learning more with AI. On ChatGPT, OpenAI’s October 2026 Signals study tracked 165 distinct work activities across nearly a hundred countries and found work usage growing broader over time rather than concentrating into programming.

Where non-coding dialogue hits hardest

Serious non-technical work is rarely a single command. In Anthropic’s data, writing an article in chat took a median of thirteen rounds of back-and-forth, against one prompt in a coding agent, and higher-paid roles stayed in conversation 1.5 times longer. Four areas reward that kind of dialogue straight away:

  • Strategy: Put your plan on the table and tell the model to pull it apart from the view of your toughest competitor or regulator.
  • Learning: Ask it to teach you an unfamiliar technical, clinical or financial topic from first principles, then test you until you can explain it plainly yourself.
  • Law and policy: Map obligations across a long contract or regulation and prepare sharp questions before sitting down with qualified counsel.
  • Critical thinking: Hand over your own draft memo and ask which claims rest on wishful thinking rather than evidence.

Your question sets the ceiling

Anthropic’s January 2026 report uncovered a striking mirror effect: across 117 countries and all 50 U.S. states, the sophistication of the model’s answer matched the sophistication of the user’s prompt almost one-for-one (0.93 correlation). And talking a problem through across turns stretched the complexity of tasks Claude could handle reliably from 3.5 hours on isolated one-shot calls to roughly 19 hours.

Coding tools deserve their success. Just do not leave the boardroom, the classroom, the clinic and the legal team waiting outside while engineers have all the fun. Bring your hardest plain-English problem and argue it out.
