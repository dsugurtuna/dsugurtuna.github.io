Usage is not quality: what to measure when you roll out AI

Usage counts show who clicked. They do not show whether the work got better, or what it cost to check.

Ugur Tuna · 30 September 2026 · 5 minute read

When an organisation rolls out an AI tool, the first numbers to arrive are usage numbers. Active users. Prompts per week. Teams onboarded. They are easy to collect, they tend to go up, and they fit neatly on a slide.

They answer a real question: are people trying the tool? They do not answer the question that matters: is the work better, and at what total cost? Those are different questions, and they need different measures.

What usage can and cannot tell you

Usage measures behaviour, not outcomes. High usage might mean the tool is valuable. It might also mean novelty, encouragement from above, or people using it for tasks where it is weak. Low usage might mean the tool is poor. It might also mean the tool sits in the wrong place in the workflow, or that the people who would gain most have not been shown how to use it.

You cannot tell which from the count alone. So treat usage as a measure of exposure, not success. It tells you how many outputs are being produced, and so how many need to be right. Rising usage with no view of quality is rising risk, not rising value.

Define good before you measure

Start with the task, not the tool. Before you look at any AI output, write down what a good result looks like for this piece of work. Use terms someone else could check.

For a summary of evidence, that means three things. Faithfulness: does every statement match what the sources say? Coverage: does it include the points a careful reader would expect? Misstatements: does it assert anything the sources do not support? For a drafted letter, it means accurate facts, the right tone, and everything the letter is required to contain.

Then build a fixed test set that looks like the real work. Use realistic inputs, with reference answers or marking criteria agreed by people who know the task. Keep it fixed so you can re-run it when the model, prompt, or workflow changes. Some criteria can be checked in code, such as whether every citation points to an existing source. Others need judgement, from a person or from a model grader whose agreement with people you have measured.

The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications lists misinformation as a risk in its own right [1]. In the 2026 edition it is LLM07 (up from LLM09 in 2025), and its examples include misleading summaries and critical omissions. The entry also names overreliance as a key driver: people treat fluent, confident output as authoritative. Measuring quality directly is how you test whether that risk holds in your setting.

Count all the effort

Speed of generation is not time saved. A draft that appears in seconds but takes an hour to verify can cost more than writing it by hand.

Measure total human effort for each accepted output. That includes writing the prompt, reading the draft, checking it against sources, correcting it, and fixing any error found later. Record checking time as part of the task, not as overhead, and record it as people work. Effort reconstructed from memory on Friday afternoon is guesswork.

Two traps recur in real roll-outs. The first is timing only the generation step, which makes every tool look fast. The second is treating skipped checks as savings. If people stop checking because prose looks fluent, you have not saved effort. You have moved it into unmeasured risk.

Baselines, shadow runs, and live use

Two kinds of number often get mixed up. A baseline describes how the work performs today without the tool: how long it takes, how often it needs correction, and which errors slip through. Without a baseline, “better” has nothing to beat.

A test result describes how the AI-assisted workflow performs on a defined task set under controlled conditions—for example, a champion-versus-challenger comparison or a shadow run scored with risk-stratified human sampling. Controlled results are repeatable, but they are not live production outcomes where inputs are messier and habits drift. When an AI tool feeds into formal policy or operational decisions, structure the comparison along the lines of HM Treasury’s Magenta Book [2].

Putting quality and effort in the same table

In evidence-synthesis-eval (built on the UK AI Security Institute’s Inspect framework [3]), four mechanics prevent evaluation scores from hiding real operational costs:

  • Deterministic claim splitting: plain code splits sentences and resolves citations so the unit of judgement cannot shift when prompts change.
  • Importance-weighted coverage (1–3): domain reviewers assign weights upfront, so omitting a primary null finding counts far more than omitting a minor detail.
  • Version-hashed rubrics and visible judge failures: every score stores the rubric hash, and unparsed judge outputs stay as NaN rather than quietly rounding to zero.
  • Timestamped effort logs: reviewers log checking and correcting minutes live against blinded items, putting quality and human time side by side.

Report small samples honestly

Early evaluations are small, and that is fine. Report counts alongside percentages: writing “seven of ten summaries had no misstatements” shows how much weight the evidence bears, whereas “70%” hides it. Show failures with examples, give uncertainty intervals, and state plainly what was not tested—task types, user groups, document lengths, or languages.

A short checklist

Before you report on an AI roll-out, check that you can answer six questions:

  1. What does a good output look like for this task, and who agreed that?
  2. What is the baseline without the tool?
  3. How good are the outputs against a fixed test set, and what failed?
  4. What is the total human effort per accepted output, including checking and rework?
  5. How many cases support each figure, and what was left untested?
  6. Is the depth of evaluation proportionate to the risk?

When you can answer those, usage figures become useful context. Without them, usage counts are just a bigger slide on a shaky foundation.
