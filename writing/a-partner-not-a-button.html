Writing

A partner, not a button

AI works best when you spend time building a working relationship with it. Full automation turns you into a button-clicking clerk.

Ugur Tuna · 4 minute read

Spend half an hour watching someone run a fully automated AI agent and look at what their job has become. A small box pops up on screen. They skim three lines. They click Approve. Forty seconds later another box appears, and they click Approve again.

They were sold the dream of managing a digital workforce. In practice, they have become the AI’s servant: a button-clicker keeping a machine moving while their own thinking switches off.

AI is a persona you learn over time

A good AI model is closer to a working persona than an office appliance. Every model has habits. One agrees with weak ideas too politely. Another writes sharp analysis if you challenge it hard, and bland filler if you leave it loose.

Getting serious work out of it takes time, much like learning how to work with a capable new colleague. Over weeks of real back-and-forth, you work out where to trust it, when to interrupt it and how to shape the exchange around the way you think best.

Real usage data backs this up, and it surprised even the people building the models. When Anthropic studied two million chats in its March 2026 Learning curves report, its researchers expected long-term users to lean harder into hands-off automation. The opposite happened. Users with at least six months of experience used one-shot “do this for me” commands nearly nine percentage points less than newcomers (29.4% against 38.1%). Experienced users argued back and forth more, asked more learning questions and succeeded roughly four percentage points more often on the exact same task.

Why effortless tools fool our internal clock

People still chase one-click automation for a very human reason: our brains confuse less mental effort with less time.

In a May 2026 study of 1,237 adults across twenty-four everyday tasks, researchers at Stanford, MIT and NYU measured both actual clock time and how hard the work felt. People predicted AI would save more than a minute per task. In reality, AI was faster on only three of the twenty-four tasks, and on a logic problem it made people nearly two minutes slower. Yet it felt easier on fifteen of them, and regular AI users were fooled just as badly as beginners. When thinking stops hurting, we assume we are flying.

Ten minutes of instant answers saps human grit

Handing over the thinking step dulls persistence remarkably fast. Across three randomised trials of 1,222 adults (COLM 2026), Grace Liu, Brian Christian and colleagues gave people an AI sidebar for ten to fifteen minutes and then switched it off. On reading questions, people who had just used AI skipped questions eight times more often than the unassisted group (8% against 1%), and their accuracy dropped from 89% to 76%.

The twist came inside the maths trial. Sixty-one per cent of users asked the AI straight for finished answers, and their unaided scores fell below their own starting mark. The twenty-seven per cent who used the AI as a sparring partner, asking only for hints and clarifications, kept their accuracy intact (76% against 77% unassisted) and quit least of anyone in the study (5%).

As Margaret Mitchell, Avijit Ghosh and Samir Passi warn in AI Agents Push Humans Out of the Loop, passive oversight degrades the overseer. Endless permission pop-ups breed approval fatigue, turning skilled engineers and analysts into tired clerks signing off work they feel detached from.

Automate chores, never judgement

Routine mechanical chores deserve automation: moving structured records, checking file formats, sorting clear-cut tickets under strict rules. Automate those cleanly in plain code where possible.

Whenever a task touches strategy, professional judgement, risk or your own craft, keep the relationship active:

  1. Form your own rough view for two minutes before opening the chat.
  2. Ask the model to challenge your reasoning or explain a gap rather than write the finished answer in one click.
  3. Treat suspiciously smooth agreement as a cue to push harder.
  4. Prefer editable drafts over autonomous agents that ask you to rubber-stamp live actions.

Spend time building a real working relationship with AI and it will multiply what you can produce. Reduce yourself to pressing Approve on an agent’s queue, and you are working for the machine.
