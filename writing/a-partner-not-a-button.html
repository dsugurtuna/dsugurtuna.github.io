A partner, not a button: keeping judgement in AI workflows

Permission pop-ups on multi-step agents turn reviewers into button-clickers. Better workflow boundaries put reversible drafts and verifiable previews ahead of rubber stamps.

Ugur Tuna · 22 November 2026 · 3 minute read

Watch what happens when someone hands a complex task to an unguided agent loop. Every minute or two, a small box pops up asking for permission to continue. Because the human has not worked through the underlying sources, they hold little context in mind. They skim a three-line summary, click Approve, wait forty seconds, and click Approve again.

That is not leadership, and it is barely supervision. The agent sets the pace, picks the path, and frames the options. Meanwhile, the person stamps boxes they no longer follow. As Margaret Mitchell, Avijit Ghosh, and Samir Passi warn in AI Agents Push Humans Out of the Loop, endless permission gates breed approval fatigue—leaving reviewers holding accountability for plausible mistakes they waved through [1].

Why easy feels fast—even when it is slower

One-click automation stays popular because human intuition confuses lower mental strain with faster completion.

In May 2026, Sunny Yu and colleagues studied 1,237 adults across twenty-four tasks, tracking both clock time and perceived effort [2]. Participants expected AI to save over a minute per task. In reality, AI was faster on only three tasks—and nearly two minutes slower on logic—yet felt easier on fifteen. Regular users fell for this speedup illusion just as badly as beginners.

Skipping the thinking step also erodes persistence. Across three trials with 1,222 adults at COLM 2026, Grace Liu, Brian Christian, and colleagues gave users an AI sidebar for ten to fifteen minutes before switching it off [3]. Among those who took finished answers, unaided question skipping jumped eightfold (8% vs 1%), and accuracy fell from 89% to 76%. By contrast, the 27% who asked only for hints and clarifications kept accuracy intact (76% vs 77% unassisted) with just a 5% quit rate.

Experienced users iterate more, not less

A common assumption says beginners chat back and forth while experienced operators automate in one command. Usage records show the opposite. Analysing two million chats in its March 2026 Learning curves report, Anthropic found that 6-month users issued one-shot commands nearly nine points less often than newcomers (29.4% vs 38.1%) [4]. Experienced users iterated more, asked diagnostic questions, and succeeded roughly four points more often.

Designing the human-in-the-loop boundary properly

None of this argues against agent automation. Mechanical plumbing—format conversion, schema checks, clean transfers—belongs in deterministic code with no pop-ups. The failure mode is putting a generic “Approve?” box in front of high-context, judgement-heavy actions.

Sound agent architectures separate workflow modes by reversibility and reviewer context:

Workflow pattern Best fit Required safeguard
Automated (allow) Read-only retrieval and deterministic checks Strict argument schemas, rate limits, audit log
Draft-by-default (draft) Briefings, replies, analyses owned by a person Tool returns an editable preview; never executes send
Queued approval (approve) External sends, record writes, paid actions Full-argument payload view, max_pending cap, TTL

In agent-guardrails, two design choices turn oversight from rubber-stamping into genuine control. First, use draft mode whenever a human should own the final wording: a draft gives a tired reviewer nothing to wave through. Second, when an action needs approve mode, cap pending items (max_pending) and inspect actual recipients and raw arguments (queue show)—never an agent summary—before an execution-time digest check runs the tool.

Automate plumbing completely. Keep judgement—both in dialogue and in system architecture—squarely in human hands.
