A partner, not a button: why full automation turns you into the AI’s servant

Good AI work grows out of a working relationship built over time. Remove the conversation and you are left clicking approve on decisions you no longer own.

Ugur Tuna · 8 minute read

Picture two people sitting at their desks on a Tuesday morning.

The first is in the middle of a sharp, messy conversation with an AI model. They sketch an argument, push back when the model plays it too safe, ask it to attack their own premise from a sceptical CFO’s point of view, cut half of what it suggests and keep one angle they had not seen before. Half an hour later, the strategy note on the screen is unmistakably theirs — only clearer, faster and better stress-tested than it would have been alone.

The second has turned on an “end-to-end autonomous agent”. Every ninety seconds, a small dialogue box pops up asking permission to run a tool, edit a file or send a draft. They glance at a fluent three-line summary, hit Approve, glance at the next one, hit Approve again. On paper, they have been promoted to a manager of digital workers. In reality, they have become a button-clicker — an attendant serving the rhythm of a machine whose reasoning they can no longer follow.

That contrast is the quiet fault line of modern AI. The industry keeps selling full, hands-off automation as the pinnacle of maturity. My view is almost the exact opposite: an AI works like a distinct persona, and getting serious value from it requires spending time building a working relationship. Strip away that interaction, and you do not become the master of the system. You become its servant.

AI is a persona you learn to work with

Think about how you work with a sharp new colleague. On day one, you do not hand them the keys to your biggest client and walk out of the room. You spend weeks learning how their mind works — where they are brilliant, where they jump ahead too fast, what kind of brief brings out their best work and what makes them waffle. Over time, a shorthand forms. You know when to let them run and when to interrupt.

Working well with an AI model follows the same human grain. Every frontier model has tendencies: a preferred cadence, a bias towards agreement, a habit of smoothing over uncertainty unless you press it. Spending time with it — calibrating how you question it, discovering which framing unlocks crisp thinking, adapting it to the way you actually produce your best work — is an investment that compounds.

What is striking is that the real-world data now backs this up, even surprising the labs themselves. When Anthropic analysed two million conversations for its March 2026 Economic Index report, its researchers had previously assumed that seasoned power users would graduate towards hands-off automation. They found the reverse. Users who had been working with Claude for six months or longer used fire-and-forget “directive” automation 8.7 percentage points less than newer users (29.4% versus 38.1%). Instead, veterans spent more time in back-and-forth iteration, learning and validation — and succeeded roughly four percentage points more often even when compared against newcomers tackling the exact same task. Early adopters are naturally self-selected, as the authors honestly note, yet the direction is unmistakable: you do not outgrow conversation with AI. You grow into it.

Why our brains keep falling for the one-click promise

If genuine dialogue works so much better, why is full automation so seductive? Because human psychology has a blind spot built right into it: we mistake the relief of not thinking for time saved.

In a May 2026 study of 1,237 US adults across twenty-four everyday cognitive tasks, researchers at Stanford, MIT and NYU uncovered what they called the “speedup illusion”. Participants predicted that AI would shave more than a minute off their tasks (68.5 seconds), missing actual AI-assisted completion times by nearly a full minute (57.8 seconds). In truth, AI made people significantly faster on only three of the twenty-four tasks — and on a logic puzzle it actually made them nearly two minutes slower (110.7 seconds) by burying them in dense formal notation. Yet AI lowered how mentally effortful the work felt on fifteen of the twenty-four tasks. Even heavy daily AI users were just as fooled as beginners.

That single dissociation explains half the hype around autonomous agents. When a workflow feels effortless, our internal stopwatch tells us we are flying, even when the clock says otherwise. We trade active thought for subjective comfort — and call it productivity.

How fast passive help erodes human grit

The cost of that trade shows up much faster than most leaders realise. It is not a slow generational drift twenty years from now; it begins inside a single coffee break.

In three randomised trials involving 1,222 adults (COLM 2026), Grace Liu, Brian Christian and colleagues gave participants an AI assistant for just ten to fifteen minutes before switching it off. Once the sidebar disappeared, the people who had relied on AI did not merely score worse — they gave up far faster. On reading comprehension questions, they skipped eight times as often as the unassisted group (8% versus 1%), and accuracy fell from 89% to 76%.

Even more revealing was what happened when the researchers split users by how they had interacted with the model during a maths task. The 61% who asked for direct answers fell below their own starting baseline once the AI was removed. By contrast, the 27% who used the model as a thinking partner — asking for hints and clarifications rather than finished answers — suffered none of the damage: they matched the unassisted group’s accuracy (76% versus 77%) and posted the lowest quit rate of anyone in the study (5%). That split was self-selected rather than separately randomised, so personality plays a part, yet the psychological mechanism is plain. A good human mentor knows when not to hand you the answer. An autonomous agent never says no, and after a few effortless minutes, normal thinking suddenly feels intolerably heavy.

How oversight quietly flips the leash

Proponents of full automation have a ready reply: “Keep a human in the loop to approve the agent’s work.” It sounds reassuring on a governance slide. In practice, turning a skilled thinker into an approval clerk is the fastest way to hollow out the loop.

In their November 2026 paper AI Agents Push Humans Out of the Loop, Margaret Mitchell, Avijit Ghosh and Samir Passi sum up the trap in four words: “oversight degrades the overseer.” When an autonomous agent fires off rapid multi-step actions and constant permission prompts, the human slips into approval fatigue — fast, skim-and-click judgements rather than deep scrutiny. Developers in early agent studies describe feeling reduced to “babysitting the outputs”, growing cognitively distant from code they are nonetheless expected to sign off. Worse still, when tired humans rubber-stamp smooth summaries and those approvals feed back into evaluations, systems get rewarded for looking reassuring rather than being right.

That is what I mean by becoming the AI’s servant. A servant does not set the direction or wrestle with the trade-offs; a servant stands beside the conveyor belt, clicking the green button so the machine can keep running, while quietly carrying the legal and reputational blame whenever it drops something on the floor.

Where automation does belong — and where it does not

None of this is an argument against automation itself. Good organisations have been automating routine drudgery for decades, and AI extends that frontier further. Deterministic data cleaning, structured format conversions, routing predictable tickets with clear rules and reversible drafts — automate those gladly, with hard guardrails around them.

The line sits between mechanical execution and judgement work. Whenever a task depends on strategy, taste, legal exposure, clinical safety, customer trust or your own developing expertise, full hands-off automation is the wrong ambition. Your goal is not to remove your mind from the loop. Your goal is to put a stronger thinking partner beside it.

Keeping the steering wheel

Building a genuine working relationship with AI is simpler — and more demanding — than installing another autonomous agent:

  1. Stay in the conversation on work that matters. Use the model to argue with your draft, surface blind spots and test scenarios rather than asking for finished answers in one click.
  2. Notice when the work feels suspiciously smooth. Effortless agreement is a warning sign, not proof of quality. Ask what assumptions the model just made on your behalf.
  3. Protect unassisted thinking. Form your own rough position before opening the chat, so you have a standard to judge the machine’s view against.
  4. Reserve autonomy for bounded, checkable chores. When an agent does act, prefer editable drafts and hard code-level limits over endless “Approve?” pop-ups that numb your attention.

Technology has always offered the same fork in the road: tools that enlarge a human being’s reach, and machinery that shrinks the human into a lever-puller. Spend the time to build a real working relationship with AI, and it will multiply what you can think and build. Turn it into a button you tap without thinking, and sooner or later it will be the machine setting the pace while you just sign for the parcels.
