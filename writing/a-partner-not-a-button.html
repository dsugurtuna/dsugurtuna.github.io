Building a working relationship with AI

An AI model is closer to a persona you learn to work with over time. Handing everything to full automation turns you into a button-clicker.

Ugur Tuna · 5 minute read

To me, an AI model feels less like a piece of office software and more like a working persona. Each model has its own habits, blind spots and tone of voice. One model is too eager to agree with whatever you say first. Another gives sharp, structured answers when you challenge it directly, and dull, safe filler when you ask lazily.

Because of that, getting your best work out of AI takes time. You have to build a working relationship with it, much like learning how to work alongside a capable new colleague. Over weeks and months, you learn how to talk to it, when to interrupt it, where it tends to bluff, and how to fit it around the way you personally think and produce.

Right now, though, much of the tech world is pushing in the opposite direction. The fashionable promise is full automation: set up an autonomous agent, press start, and step out of the way while the machine handles the whole job. On a slide deck, that sounds like freedom. In daily life, removing human interaction from thinking work does something quiet and damaging. Instead of making you the boss, it turns you into the AI’s servant.

The button-clicker trap

Watch what happens in practice when someone hands a complex piece of work to an automated agent. The agent runs a string of steps on its own, and every minute or two it throws a small box onto the screen asking for permission to carry on. The person sitting at the desk has not worked through the problem themselves, so they have very little context in their head. They skim a short summary, click Approve, wait forty seconds, and click Approve again.

That is not leadership, and it is barely even supervision. It is button-clicking. The machine sets the pace, chooses the path, and frames the options, while the human sits at the end of the belt stamping boxes they no longer truly understand. When people stay in that passive role for long, attention fades. Reviewers feel detached from the work in front of them, wave through plausible mistakes, and still carry the blame when something goes wrong.

Why easy feels fast, even when it is not

There is a simple reason in human nature why one-click automation is so tempting. Our brains are bad at telling the difference between saving time and saving mental effort.

When people guess how much time AI will save them on short everyday tasks, they regularly expect a big speedup even on jobs where the clock barely moves at all, or where sorting out a wordy AI reply actually takes longer than doing the task straight away. What AI almost always lowers is the feeling of mental strain. Because the work feels lighter, we walk away convinced we were faster. That comfort pulls people towards handing over more and more of the thinking step, even when the end result is no quicker and less their own.

Once we get used to instant answers, our patience for hard thinking drops surprisingly quickly too. Even ten or fifteen minutes of letting an AI hand out finished answers is enough to change how people behave after the tool is switched off: they give up on tough questions far more often than people who worked unaided. Yet there is an encouraging catch in the same evidence. People who use AI by asking for hints, questions and clarifications rather than finished answers keep their accuracy and stick with hard problems at least as well as anyone else. The harm does not come from using AI. It comes from letting AI replace your own involvement.

Experienced users talk more, not less

A common assumption is that beginners chat back and forth with AI while seasoned experts automate everything in a single command. Large-scale usage records show the reverse. People who have used conversational AI for half a year or more rely significantly less on one-shot “do this for me” instructions than newcomers do. Instead, experienced users spend more of their sessions iterating back and forth, asking questions to learn, checking their own work, and getting higher success rates on the exact same tasks.

That matches everything I have seen in real teams. The people doing the strongest work with AI are never the ones pressing a magic button and walking away. They are the ones arguing with the model, testing three angles on a problem, throwing half a draft in the bin, and keeping their own hands on the wheel.

Keeping the right balance

Of course, plenty of dull, mechanical chores deserve full automation. Moving clean data from one format to another, checking whether files match a strict rule, or running predictable background tasks should be automated, ideally with plain, reliable code rather than a guessing model. Nobody grows wiser by copying rows between spreadsheets by hand.

The line to guard is the line around thinking itself. Whenever a piece of work shapes a decision, builds your skill, or carries your judgement to other people, keep the conversation alive. Spend the time to learn how your model thinks, shape it to the way you work best, push back when it takes the easy road, and refuse any setup that reduces your mind to clicking an approval button for a machine.
