Assign AI roles by data, not by dice

Every major AI lab trains on a different world of data. Give one model the chair, match others by measured skill, and require proof.

Ugur Tuna · 5 minute read

If you had to make a high-stakes decision, you would not hire five clones of the same adviser, hand out job titles out of a hat and take a show of hands.

Yet most “multi-agent AI” setups do exactly that. They spin up three copies of one model, tell them to role-play as sceptic, optimist and judge over the exact same text, or mix models at random and let fluent guesswork sway the room.

Earlier, I wrote that a second model is a critic, not a verifier. To reach the best answer AI can actually give, you need two stricter rules: assign each lab’s model the role its training world and test scores earn it, and refuse to let any counter-argument change the decision without checkable evidence attached.

Different labs grow up on different worlds of data

Google, Meta, Anthropic, OpenAI and xAI are not building interchangeable copies of the same brain. Each lab is shaped by different data, different human feedback and different engineering priorities.

Google sits on decades of web indexing, search structure and YouTube transcripts. Meta understands social expression at vast scale from Facebook and Instagram. xAI trains right beside live public debate and breaking news on X. OpenAI runs the broadest stream of everyday human feedback on earth because more ordinary users talk to it than any rival. Anthropic has poured years of focused talent into careful technical reasoning and high-grade coding feedback.

You do not need secret training manifests to use that difference well. Different roots produce different strengths and different blind spots. Putting clones in a circle wastes the biggest advantage of having competing labs.

Rule one: match roles to measured strength

Instead of picking roles at random, test which model genuinely performs best at each job on your own tasks. Put one strong model in the chair as primary decision-maker to answer the main query. Assign models from other labs to focused roles where their data and evaluations give them an edge: broad retrieval, maths and logic checks, live counter-signals or option generation. Keep their first pass independent so the opening speaker does not herd everyone else down one path.

Sakana AI’s June 2026 Fugu technical report showed what happens when frontier models from OpenAI (GPT-5.5), Anthropic (Claude Opus 4.8) and Google (Gemini 3.1 Pro) are routed by measured capability and isolated before handoff. The combined team beat every single lab on its own flagship benchmarks, reaching 73.7% on SWE-Bench Pro, 82.1% on Terminal Bench 2.1 and 95.5% on GPQA-Diamond. On one server task, GPT-5.5 built the system and marked it working; Opus 4.8 audited it cold and spotted that GPT’s check had accidentally pinged a leftover server process from earlier. Handed that concrete finding, GPT fixed the bug cleanly.

Rule two: no evidence, no vote

Diverse models still fail if you let them argue from bare opinion. When a second model invents a plausible objection without proof, it often talks a right decision-maker into a wrong answer.

Three 2026 studies expose how dangerous ungrounded debate is, and how cleanly evidence fixes it:

  • Clones arguing in circles: In More Debate, Same Evidence (July 2026), Yuelyu Ji tested three copies of GPT-5.5 Chat debating the same text across 1,950 examples. Round two added almost zero new substance, changing speaker order swung accuracy by up to eight points, and on one benchmark 70.4% of changed answers turned a right answer into a wrong one.
  • Agreement rising as truth falls: In PROCLAIM (August 2026), Masnun Nuha Chowdhury and colleagues paired different model families with courtroom-style admissibility rules and live evidence lookup, beating standard debate by 10 to 16 percentage points. When they switched live evidence lookup off, accuracy dropped 7.5 points while agreement between the judges actually rose (κ climbing from 0.468 to 0.599). Left without fresh facts, models happily agree on a lie.
  • Flipping a 5-to-0 mistake: In AEGIS-D (EMNLP 2026 Findings), Jiayang Sun and colleagues required positive confirming proof rather than mere lack of objection on hard Olympiad maths, cutting false positives more than eightfold on GPT-5.2 and seventeenfold on Claude Opus 4.6. On one flawed proof citing an invented formula, all five agents initially voted 5-to-0 to accept it while admitting their check was incomplete. Because the evidence gate blocked unsupported consensus and demanded small number tests, the panel disproved the formula and flipped to 0-to-5 against.

Save the council for calls that matter

Running four or five models with source checks costs more time and tokens than a single prompt. Nobody needs a courtroom to draft a thank-you note.

Use this setup where a quiet hallucination hurts: strategy papers, legal or regulatory analysis, clinical synthesis and major technical architecture decisions. Give one tested model the chair, assign rival labs the roles their data earned them, verify every cited quote or calculation in plain code, and let only proven counter-evidence move the verdict.
